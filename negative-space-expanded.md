# Negative space — expansion (binding)

Four further points continuing the three in `## Negative space`, each at a different boundary — the brief, the measurement, the verification, the postmortem — where the load-bearing content is what gets left out. Source papers archived at `/mnt/archive4/PAPERS/Prepared/<slug>.md`.

4. **State the problem before, and free of, the solution.** A brief states the problem and the client-side criteria a solution must meet as a distinct act, before any mechanism and without the intended solution's vocabulary. A problem statement that cannot be written without leaning on the mechanism has already collapsed into the solution — the abstraction boundary is wrong and the brief is not ready to dispatch. This is the *why* behind the handoff rules (no prescribed deliverable shape, no prescribed investigation shape, no hypotheses): client-side criteria fence off non-solutions without dictating the one solution. (Lamport 1978, "State the Problem Before Describing the Solution".)

5. **A measurement's negative space is as load-bearing as the program's.** What an evaluation scrambles away, never checks exists, or silently holds constant decides the result. Evaluate against a workload that keeps the real task's structure and ordering, never a convenient surrogate that designed the structure out. Before building machinery to fix a reported problem, measure that it is real and decompose the headline metric to where the cost actually lives — the visible symptom is frequently a different cause wearing the costume of the obvious one. A factor held constant across all runs is a silent confound: vary it deliberately and report the distribution, or randomize over it. This is the rigorous form of "compilation proves nothing" and the ground under diagnose-first and `/profile`. (Wilson et al. 1995; Johnstone & Wilson 1998; Curtsinger & Berger 2013, "STABILIZER".)

6. **Build verification adversarially from the artifact's own decision points,** not from the inputs the implementer happened to imagine, and assign the adversary to an agent that did not write the code. The killer case lives in the inputs the author never enumerated — "passes the tests I thought of" is the precise blind spot, and a probe built from imagined inputs inherits it. An independent adversary built from observable decision points is what makes a probe-gate mean something. (McIlroy 1999, "A Killer Adversary for Quicksort" — treats the sort as a black box and constructs the worst-case input on the fly, driving any quicksort, even randomized, to Θ(n²).)

7. **A defect that reaches the user is a trajectory, not a root cause.** The pipeline is layered defenses (orchestrator, sub-agents, briefs, tests, canon docs, human review); a failure that gets through is a conjunction of latent gaps that lined up, so the postmortem maps the surviving trajectory across every layer, never a single root cause pinned on one agent — and a post-hoc "guard" usually adds coupling and new latent failures rather than removing the hazard. Forced near-tie judgments ("is this brief ready", "are these shapes genuinely equal", "does this diff count as done", "iterate or escalate") are metastability sites with no rule guaranteeing a correct snap answer: widen the gap with a disambiguating fact or escalate to the user, and budget a hard escalation trigger for the unbounded tail. Bounded-time correct arbitration at a genuine tie is in the negative space of every possible design — a fix promising clean bounded resolution at a real near-tie is suspect, because it smuggles back the impossible decision it claims to have solved. (Cook 1998, "How Complex Systems Fail"; Lamport 1976, "On the Glitch Phenomenon"; 1984, "Buridan's Principle".)
